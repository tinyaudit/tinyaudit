\title{TinyAudit: On-Device, Uncertainty-Aware Fairness Audits\\
that Survive (and Expose) Compression}
\author{Atharva Doke \and Kasyap Tumuluri}
\date{\today}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Machine learning is moving onto microcontroller-class hardware, where models are
aggressively compressed (pruned, quantized) to fit. Responsible-AI audits, in
contrast, are almost always run on full-precision models on a workstation, and
they typically look at fairness, uncertainty, and explainability in isolation. We
present \textsc{TinyAudit}, an auditing pipeline that measures all three together
\emph{through} compression and \emph{within} the memory and compute envelope of
the audited model itself. Using it we report two findings, each replicated and
reported as a mean over 10 seeds. First, point-prediction fairness and
calibration fairness are \emph{decoupled}: the two lenses rank sensitive groups
differently, so a model that looks fair on demographic parity need not be fair in
its per-group calibration. The result holds on \dataset{UCI Adult} and replicates
on \dataset{COMPAS}, whose favorable direction is inverted. Second, compression
can \emph{hide} unfairness: as a network is pruned it can look progressively
fairer on demographic parity precisely as its calibration collapses, because it
degrades toward a near-constant predictor. Finally, the audit is cheap enough to
live on the device it inspects: its peak working set is about \SI{0.6}{\mega\byte}
plus \SI{2.3}{\kilo\byte} per audited sample, so streamed in small batches the
whole pipeline runs under \SI{1}{\mega\byte} and \SI{0.1}{\second} irrespective
of test-set size. \textsc{TinyAudit} is released as an installable package with
full CI and a one-page audit card.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Two trends are on a collision course. The first is the migration of machine
learning onto microcontroller-class hardware -- the ``TinyML'' regime of
sub-milliwatt inference on \SIrange{32}{512}{\kilo\byte} of SRAM
\cite{somvanshi2025tinydl,heydari2025tinyml}. Fitting a model into that envelope
means compressing it: pruning weights to zero and quantizing the survivors to
int8, at compression ratios up to $49\times$ against the cloud model, trading
several points of accuracy for the memory budget \cite{heydari2025tinyml}. These
compressed models now sit in wearables, agricultural sensors, and vehicles
\cite{hayajneh2024agriculture,mohammad2025iot}, making high-stakes and
demographically-loaded decisions at the edge.

The second trend is the maturation of responsible-AI auditing. Fairness has a
well-developed metric toolkit -- demographic parity, equalized odds, disparate
impact \cite{pagano2023fairness} -- and a growing set of frameworks that
formalize the audit process \cite{maneriker2023avoir,lalor2024fairframe}. But
these tools were built for the cloud notebook. They assume workstation compute,
individual-level demographic labels at test time, and full-precision models
\cite{lee2021landscape,deng2022exploring,han2024ffb}. They also examine one axis
at a time: fairness \emph{or} uncertainty \emph{or} explainability, rarely their
interaction.

Nobody, to our knowledge, checks what compression does to a model's audit
properties, nor whether the audit itself fits the device budget. This matters
because the two trends interact in a way that is not benign. A recent thread in
the fairness literature shows that point-prediction metrics are systematically
incomplete: a model can pass a demographic-parity check while being
\emph{confidently} wrong for one group and \emph{hesitantly} correct for another
\cite{kuzucu2024uncertainty,rosenblatt2024fairlyuncertain}. Compression is
exactly the kind of operation that can move a model along that hidden axis --
degrading calibration for a minority group -- without moving the point metric an
auditor would look at.

\paragraph{The claim.} Compression-era model audits must be uncertainty-aware.
Point-fairness and calibration-fairness are decoupled; compression can move one
without the other; and, crucially, all of this can be measured within the
device's own compute budget rather than after the fact on a workstation.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{An on-device audit instrument.} A seven-stage pipeline that
    couples fairness, uncertainty, and explainability and measures them on the
    \emph{compressed} model, emitting a one-page traffic-light audit card and a
    machine-readable manifest (Section~\ref{sec:instrument}).
    \item \textbf{A decoupling result.} Point-fairness and calibration-fairness
    rank sensitive groups differently, on \dataset{Adult} and replicated on
    \dataset{COMPAS} with its favorable direction inverted
    (Section~\ref{sec:decoupling}).
    \item \textbf{Compression can hide unfairness.} Under progressive pruning,
    demographic parity and per-group calibration move in opposite directions
    (Section~\ref{sec:compression}).
    \item \textbf{A feasibility measurement.} The audit's own peak working set is
    $\approx\!\SI{0.6}{\mega\byte} + \SI{2.3}{\kilo\byte}$ per sample and is
    \emph{constant in dataset size} when streamed, so it fits the class of device
    it inspects (Section~\ref{sec:feasibility}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{The TinyAudit Instrument}
\label{sec:instrument}

Given a fitted model and a labeled test set, \textsc{TinyAudit} runs a
seven-stage pipeline and emits a one-page audit card plus a machine-readable JSON
manifest that every stage writes to. Every figure in the card is backed by a
committed CSV, and the card is generated from the manifest, never hand-edited.
The stages are:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Profile} -- parameter count, an analytic FLOPs proxy, peak RAM
    (via \texttt{tracemalloc}), and wall-clock per sample.
    \item \textbf{Point fairness} -- demographic-parity difference, equalized-odds
    difference, and disparate-impact ratio, each with an explicitly pinned
    favorable direction and a hypothesis-tested range.
    \item \textbf{Uncertainty} -- MC dropout (MLP only), a lightweight
    perturbation ensemble, and a QUTE-style early-exit estimator, each returning
    predictive entropy, variance, and mutual information.
    \item \textbf{Uncertainty-aware fairness} -- per-group predictive entropy,
    per-group expected calibration error (ECE), and a selective-fairness AUC that
    integrates parity as low-confidence predictions are progressively abstained.
    \item \textbf{Explainability} -- SHAP, LIME, and a dependency-free occlusion
    explainer, cross-checked for top-feature flips across sensitive groups.
    \item \textbf{Compression} -- int8 dynamic quantization or magnitude pruning,
    applied \emph{before} every metric above so the audit sees the deployed model.
    \item \textbf{Render} -- the one-page, traffic-light-coded card.
\end{enumerate}
The design constraint that shapes all of this is the memory envelope: the audit
is written so its working set is dominated by per-sample buffers, letting it be
streamed in small batches at a peak-RAM cost independent of test-set size
(Section~\ref{sec:feasibility}). The in-house metric implementations are the hot
path; \texttt{fairlearn} is imported only in tests, as a reference oracle, never
in the package itself.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Datasets and models.} We use \dataset{UCI Adult}
\cite{kohavi1996adult}, \dataset{COMPAS} \cite{angwin2016machinebias}, and the
\dataset{ACSIncome} subset of \dataset{Folktables} \cite{ding2021retiring} -- the
last a real 2018 California Census reconstruction that Ding et al.\ released
precisely because the 1994 \dataset{Adult} data no longer reflects contemporary
distributions. Models are standard scikit-learn estimators: logistic regression,
a two-hidden-layer MLP ($32\times32$), and a decision tree, each fit on
\texttt{StandardScaler}d features. Scaling is not cosmetic: without it the MLP on
\dataset{Adult} sits near the majority-class rate ($\approx\!0.54$ accuracy);
with it, $\approx\!0.84$. Sensitive attributes are \texttt{sex} and
\texttt{race}.

\paragraph{Reproducibility.} A single \texttt{seed} flag seeds every RNG
(\texttt{random}, \texttt{numpy}, \texttt{torch}, and \texttt{PYTHONHASHSEED}).
All headline numbers are reported as mean $\pm$ standard deviation over seeds
0--9 (\texttt{experiments/run\_multiseed.py}); every result CSV is stamped with
the seed, a config hash, and resolved library versions, so a CSV is
self-describing. Determinism is guaranteed on Linux/x86; minor float drift
elsewhere is expected, which is exactly why we report std columns rather than
point estimates.

\paragraph{Baselines.} Table~\ref{tab:baselines} confirms the models are credible
(published-range accuracy), so the fairness findings sit on top of models that
actually work rather than on degenerate predictors.

\section{Finding 1: The Fairness Lenses Decouple}
\label{sec:decoupling}

Our first finding is that the two ways of asking ``is this model fair?'' -- how
often each group gets the favorable prediction (point fairness) and how honest
the model's confidence is for each group (calibration fairness) -- do not agree.
They rank the sensitive groups differently, so an auditor who looks only at the
point metric is blind to a real disparity in a second one.

On \dataset{Adult} by \texttt{race} (Table~\ref{tab:decoup-adult}), the
best-calibrated group by a wide margin -- White, ECE $0.008 \pm 0.002$, three to
seven times lower than every other group -- is also one of the most-selected. The
selection-rate ranking and the calibration ranking do not line up: the smaller
groups carry higher, noisier calibration error that the selection view never
surfaces. By \texttt{sex}, the story flips in the other direction: the Male/Female
gap is large in selection rate ($0.253 \pm 0.007$ vs $0.079 \pm 0.004$) but
negligible in calibration ($0.009$ vs $0.014$). Whichever single lens an auditor
picks, it hides the disparity the other one sees.

The result replicates on \dataset{COMPAS} (Table~\ref{tab:decoup-compas}), where
the positive outcome is being flagged high-risk, so higher is \emph{worse}.
African-American defendants are flagged at roughly twice the rate of the next
group ($0.441$ vs $0.249$) -- the well-known point-fairness disparity -- yet the
worst-calibrated group is one flagged far less often. Point parity and
calibration parity again single out different groups, now on a dataset whose
favorable direction is inverted, so the decoupling is not an
\dataset{Adult}-specific artifact of one label convention.

The practical reading is a design principle for edge audits: because
sensitive-attribute labels are rarely available at inference time, on-device
audit triggers tend to lean on confidence scores as a proxy. If point parity and
calibration parity disagree about which group is worst served, a
confidence-thresholded trigger applied uniformly across groups can silently track
the wrong disparity \cite{kenfack2023fairness}. Reporting both lenses is the
cheap fix.

\section{Finding 2: Compression Can Hide Unfairness}
\label{sec:compression}

Our second finding is that compression does not merely trade accuracy for size --
it can rewrite the audit. As the \dataset{Adult} MLP is pruned from uncompressed
to 90\% sparsity (Table~\ref{tab:compression}, Figure~\ref{fig:compression}), its
demographic-parity gap net \emph{falls} ($0.175 \rightarrow 0.014$) while
per-group calibration error net \emph{climbs} roughly tenfold
($0.027 \rightarrow 0.282$). The model looks progressively fairer on the metric
an auditor is most likely to report, precisely as its calibration collapses,
because heavy pruning degrades it toward a near-constant predictor: a model that
says ``no'' to everyone has zero demographic-parity gap and is uniformly
miscalibrated. Parity improved for the worst possible reason.

This is an \emph{endpoint} contrast, and we are careful to state it as one. The
intermediate pruning levels are seed-noisy -- the parity standard deviation runs
from $\pm 0.06$ to $\pm 0.10$, wide enough that the middle rows overlap -- so the
robust claim is the \emph{direction} between the endpoints, not a smooth
monotone curve. Figure~\ref{fig:compression} shows the $\pm 1$ std bands crossing
in the middle for exactly this reason; the endpoints are what carry the finding.
An auditor watching only demographic parity through a compression sweep would
read the trend as improving fairness. The uncertainty lens is what reveals it as
degradation.

\section{On-Device Feasibility}
\label{sec:feasibility}

An audit that cannot run on the device it inspects is a workstation tool with a
TinyML label. The differentiating question is therefore not the audited model's
footprint -- that is well known -- but the audit's own. We profiled the entire
pipeline (fairness, uncertainty, and explainability together) with
\texttt{tracemalloc}, which observes NumPy buffer allocations, so the number is a
real working-set proxy rather than a bookkeeping count. It excludes the
interpreter itself, which is the correct exclusion: the comparison of interest is
against a compiled on-device implementation of the same arithmetic, not against
CPython.

The central result is that the audit's peak memory is governed by the
\emph{batch size} it processes, not by the size of the test set. The working set
is dominated by per-sample buffers -- the feature matrix, the ensemble's stacked
prediction arrays, the explainer's perturbation batch -- so it scales linearly in
the batch and is otherwise a fixed overhead
(Table~\ref{tab:audit-footprint}). Across datasets and models we measure

\begin{equation}
\text{peak RAM} \;\approx\; \SI{0.6}{\mega\byte} \;+\; \SI{2.3}{\kilo\byte}
\times (\text{batch size}),
\label{eq:footprint}
\end{equation}

fit from the endpoints of the \dataset{Adult} sweep ($625{,}005$ B at batch 64 to
$10{,}061{,}660$ B at batch 4096). The consequence is the feasibility claim: an
arbitrarily large test set can be streamed through the audit in fixed-size
batches at a \emph{constant} peak-RAM cost. At a 256-row streaming batch the whole
three-method audit peaks below \SI{1}{\mega\byte} -- \SI{0.64}{\mega\byte} for the
\dataset{Adult} logistic model, \SI{0.95}{\mega\byte} for the MLP,
\SI{0.61}{\mega\byte} for either \dataset{COMPAS} model -- and completes in under
\SI{0.1}{\second}. That fits comfortably on a Cortex-M7-class device and is a
$10\times$ margin below a single \SI{4096}{}-row pass.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\textsc{TinyAudit} sits where three fast-moving literatures meet, plus a fourth
that binds them. Each has matured since 2021, yet they remain largely
disconnected: edge ML rarely measures fairness, fairness toolkits assume
cloud-scale compute, and explainability research privileges accuracy over
deployability.

\paragraph{On-device ML and its auditing gap.} TinyML runs inference on
microcontrollers with \SIrange{32}{512}{\kilo\byte} of SRAM and sub-milliwatt
budgets \cite{somvanshi2025tinydl}. Surveys document sub-\SI{5}{\milli\second}
latencies and up-to-$49\times$ compression against cloud models, with accuracy
dropping from $\approx\!95\%$ to $\approx\!85\%$ at the edge
\cite{heydari2025tinyml}, across healthcare wearables, ecological sensing, and
agriculture \cite{hayajneh2024agriculture,mohammad2025iot}. Auditing
infrastructure has not kept pace: Somvanshi et al.\ name ``the absence of robust
benchmarks for TinyDL systems'' as a core open problem, noting that MLPerf Tiny
and EEMBC MLMark measure latency, accuracy, and energy but not fairness or
explainability \cite{somvanshi2025tinydl}, and Heydari and Mahmoud warn of a
publication bias that overestimates deployment capability
\cite{heydari2025tinyml}. Fairness-aware edge model search
\cite{sheng2022fahana} and lightweight embedded interpretability
\cite{alnusif2025xai} are among the few works inside this gap.
\textsc{TinyAudit} targets it directly: an audit sized for the SRAM budgets these
surveys describe.

\paragraph{Fairness auditing and its cloud-scale assumptions.} The group-fairness
toolkit -- demographic parity, equalized odds, disparate impact -- is well
developed \cite{pagano2023fairness}, though non-experts often misinterpret it
\cite{saha2019measuring} and a century of psychological auditing practice argues
for triangulating rather than trusting a single metric
\cite{landers2022auditing}. Frameworks formalize the process
\cite{maneriker2023avoir,tang2023trustless,lalor2024fairframe}, and \dataset{UCI
Adult} and \dataset{COMPAS} are near-universal benchmarks
\cite{fabris2022datasets}, with \dataset{Folktables} released to retire the
1994 \dataset{Adult} distribution \cite{ding2021retiring}. The consequential gap
is toolkit design. Studies of AIF360, Fairlearn, and Aequitas find limited
end-to-end coverage, a uniform assumption of test-time demographic labels, and a
gap between academic use cases and deployment reality
\cite{lee2021landscape,deng2022exploring}; benchmarking work adds that AIF360
supports only two groups for post-processing, Fairlearn offers no validation
split, and both require group information at test time
\cite{han2024ffb}. These are cloud-notebook instruments, which is why a
lightweight, label-frugal alternative is necessary rather than merely convenient.

\paragraph{Explainability under constraints.} SHAP and LIME dominate but are
costly and fragile at the edge: they produce different top-feature rankings on
the same data, are sensitive to feature collinearity, and carry exponential
(SHAP) or unstable (LIME) cost \cite{salih2025explainable,sufian2024xai}. Recent
work adapts XAI to embedded contexts through pruning, quantization, and
approximation \cite{nguyen2025xedgeai,alnusif2025xai}, confirming that
interpretability on small models is feasible but requires design choices
general-purpose toolkits do not automate. \textsc{TinyAudit} ships an
occlusion explainer with no heavy dependencies alongside SHAP and LIME for this
reason.

\paragraph{Uncertainty entangled with fairness.} The binding thread, and the
origin of our decoupling result, is that point-prediction fairness ignores the
uncertainty beneath the predictions. Kuzucu et al.\ show a model can look fair on
point metrics while exhibiting biased \emph{uncertainty} against a group, and
introduce aleatoric/epistemic fairness measures \cite{kuzucu2024uncertainty}.
Rosenblatt and Witter's FairlyUncertain benchmark finds that abstaining under
high uncertainty lowers error but does not fix group imbalance, and that
well-calibrated uncertainty can improve fairness with no explicit intervention
\cite{rosenblatt2024fairlyuncertain}. On the engineering side, QUTE delivers
uncertainty quantification on KB-sized devices via an early-exit-assisted
ensemble ($59\%$ smaller, $3.2\times$ fewer FLOPs than prior work) but never
evaluates those estimates through a fairness lens \cite{ghanathe2024qute}.
Finally, enforcing fairness only where the predicted sensitive attribute is
high-confidence yields better trade-offs under missing demographics
\cite{kenfack2023fairness}. No existing system combines on-device uncertainty
estimation with fairness auditing in one lightweight pipeline; that is the
narrow, actionable gap \textsc{TinyAudit} fills.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Features are standardized before fitting; without it the MLP is
    unusable, so our numbers describe the scaled-input regime.
    \item The uncertainty ``ensemble'' is five lightly-jittered copies of the one
    (compressed) model, not five from-scratch retrains, so uncertainty magnitudes
    should be read as relative, not absolute.
    \item int8 models' float weights are unreachable, so their uncertainty columns
    are blank; decision trees have no dense weights and sit out the compression
    grid.
    \item Heavily-pruned \dataset{COMPAS} (only $\approx 5$ features) degenerates
    to a near-constant predictor; those cells are a corner case, not a trend.
    \item The audit-footprint numbers exclude the CPython interpreter and are
    measured on x86; an actual MCU port is future work, so
    Equation~\ref{eq:footprint} is a feasibility bound, not a deployment
    benchmark.
    \item Exact reproducibility is guaranteed on Linux/x86; elsewhere expect
    last-digit float drift, which the std columns absorb.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Audits written for the cloud do not transfer safely to the compressed models
running at the edge. We showed that point-fairness and calibration-fairness
decouple -- ranking sensitive groups differently on \dataset{Adult} and on
\dataset{COMPAS} with its direction inverted -- and that compression can drive
demographic parity toward zero while calibration collapses, hiding degradation as
apparent fairness. Both findings are only visible when fairness is audited
\emph{with} uncertainty, and both are reported as means over 10 seeds so the
direction, not a lucky seed, carries the claim. Finally, the audit that reveals
them is cheap enough to live on the device it inspects: a sub-megabyte,
sub-tenth-of-a-second working set that is constant in test-set size when
streamed. \textsc{TinyAudit} is released as an installable package with full CI, a
one-page audit card, and every result regenerable from a committed script.
